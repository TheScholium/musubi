# Zettelkasten and the Musubi philosophy

A Zettelkasten is a collection of small notes. Each note holds one idea, has an address that does not change, and links to other notes. The German word means "slip box". The method is famous through one German sociologist, but the practice is much older and much wider than one person or one continent. This essay tells that wider history, explains why the method suits mathematics and physics in particular, collects what people who use it today report, in several languages, and shows how each part of Musubi follows from all of this.

## 1. The method in one paragraph

The best documented Zettelkasten is the card index of Niklas Luhmann (1927–1998), a sociologist at Bielefeld University. It holds about 90,000 handwritten cards in two collections. The first collection (about 23,000 cards, from about 1951 to 1962) has 108 numbered sections; the second (about 67,000 cards) has a bibliography of about 15,000 references and a keyword index of about 3,200 entries.[^luhmann-cards] Luhmann said that he did not do all of his thinking alone: *"Ich denke ja nicht alles allein"*. Much of it, he said, happened in the card index.[^luhmann-folder] His archive describes the box as a "surprise generator" (*Überraschungsgenerator*): a structure that shows its owner connections that the owner did not plan.[^luhmann-schmidt]

The rules are simple:

1. **One idea per note.** A note is small enough to link to as a whole.
2. **Write it in your own words.** Copying is collection, not understanding.
3. **Give it a permanent address.** Links must survive reorganisation.
4. **Link it to what it depends on, extends or contradicts.** The links are the knowledge.
5. **Let structure grow from the links, not from folders decided in advance.**

None of these rules started in Bielefeld.

## 2. An old practice in many places

### Knots before writing

The oldest note systems were knots. A commentary on the Chinese *Book of Changes* (the *Xici*) says that in the highest antiquity people governed with knotted cords (結繩), and that later sages replaced the cords with written signs.[^xici] Modern scholarship reads the Chinese cord records as an aid to memory for counting and checking, not as writing.[^szigethy]

In the Andes, the *khipu* (Quechua for "knot") recorded both numbers and narratives. The first known khipus come from the Wari civilisation, before the Inca.[^khipu-google] A typical khipu has a primary cord with pendant cords; pendants can carry subsidiary cords, which can carry further subsidiaries. The largest have more than 1,500 pendants and six levels of subsidiaries.[^khipu-discover] That is a tree of records with branches that hang from branches: in structure, a structure note.

The name of this program comes from the same image. *Musubi* (結び) is Japanese for a knot or a tie. A Zettelkasten is a set of ties.

### China: brush notes and the record of daily knowledge

*Biji* (筆記, "brush notes") is a genre of classical Chinese writing made of scattered notes: anecdotes, quotations, philological speculation, observations, anything the author thought worth recording. It appeared in the Wei and Jin periods and matured in the Tang.[^biji]

The most famous scientific example is the *Mengxi bitan* (夢溪筆談, *Brush Talks from Dream Brook*) of Shen Kuo (1031–1095), published in 1088. It covers mathematics, astronomy, medicine, optics, engineering, metallurgy and early archaeology.[^dreampool] Shen gave the first known description of the magnetic needle compass in it.[^shenkuo] He explained the title himself: in retirement he had only his brush and ink slab to converse with.[^shine]

Six centuries later, Gu Yanwu (1613–1682) kept the notes that became the *Rizhilu* (日知錄, *Record of Daily Knowledge*), more than a thousand entries on philosophy, government, examinations, customs, astronomy and geography. In his preface he wrote that he had noted down what he perceived since childhood, revised a note again and again when it turned out to be wrong, and deleted it entirely when he found that earlier scholars had already said it.[^rizhilu] Those three habits (record, revise, remove what is not yours) are the discipline of a permanent note.

### Korea: Dasan's method of excerpts

Jeong Yak-yong (丁若鏞, 1762–1836), known as Dasan, left a collected works of more than five hundred volumes.[^dasan-kci] He trained his sons in *chosŏ* (鈔書), a method of reading by excerpting: he gave them a table of contents and a set of rules, and they copied out passages in one fixed direction, so that they learned what reading with a focus can produce. Dasan himself wrote notes from his excerpts, added his own thoughts, and built books from them.[^dasan-method] Among the precepts that the scholar Chung Min draws from Dasan's practice: separate the grain from the chaff among your notes, and arrange what remains by system.[^dasan-kormedi]

### Japan: one card, one item

In 1969 the ethnologist Umesao Tadao (梅棹忠夫, 1920–2010) published *Chiteki seisan no gijutsu* (知的生産の技術, *The Art of Intellectual Production*). The book introduced the B6 "Kyoto University card" (京大式カード) and went through more than a hundred printings.[^umesao-atomi] Its chapter headings could serve as the rules of a modern Zettelkasten: *one card, one item* (一枚一項目), *write in order to forget* (わすれるためにかく), and *classification is not the goal* (分類が目的ではない).[^umesao-toc] Umesao's point was that cards exist to be rearranged, and that rearranging them shows connections between cards that seemed unrelated.[^umesao-array] He came to cards from fieldwork: he cut up his field notebooks into cards so that he could reorder the material.[^umesao-hokox]

His colleague Kawakita Jirō (川喜田二郎, 1920–2009) developed the KJ method from fieldwork in the Nepal Himalaya. He designed a data card for field records, built theory from the data upwards, and described the method in *Hassōhō* (発想法, 1967).[^kj-jstage] He saw it as an alternative to Western quantitative methods in ethnography.[^kj-wiki] The method lives on worldwide as the "affinity diagram".[^affinity]

### The Islamic world: captured thoughts, glosses on glosses

Ibn al-Jawzi (c. 1116–1201) of Baghdad kept a book of thoughts that occurred to him during study and daily life, and named it *Ṣayd al-khāṭir* (صيد الخاطر), "the hunting of passing thoughts". In its introduction he says that the thoughts that came to him were too precious to let pass, so he made a place to keep them.[^jawzi] That is the fleeting note, eight centuries before the word.

The madrasa tradition also built networks of commentary. A base text received a *sharḥ* (commentary), the commentary received a *ḥāshiya* (gloss or supercommentary), and glosses received glosses in turn. Zamakhsharī's Qurʾān commentary, the *Kashshāf*, became a madrasa textbook and started a whole tradition of supercommentaries.[^kashshaf] In printed editions the gloss surrounds the text it explains, adding clarifications, references, corrections and further questions.[^dasuqi] In Mughal India, Persian *sharḥ* and *ḥāshiya* served as the machinery of teaching law.[^mughal] Ottoman readers kept *mecmua*, personal miscellanies that could be anthologies, scrapbooks or almanacs.[^mecmua]

In West Africa, the manuscripts of Timbuktu (estimates range from about 100,000 to about 350,000 items) carry marginalia of their own: corrections, additions, clarifications and independent notes, some of them peculiar to the region.[^timbuktu-wiki][^timbuktu-margin] Signed and dated marginal notes in the Fondo Kati collection record contemporary events and family history.[^timbuktu-kati] The margin was a note system, and readers linked their notes to the exact passage that prompted them.

### South Asia: the thread and its commentaries

Pāṇini's grammar of Sanskrit, the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (c. 4th century BCE), states the whole system in about 4,000 *sūtras*, very concise statements.[^panini-heid][^vedic] *Sūtra* means "thread". Later scholars added *vārttikas* (critical notes) and *bhāṣyas* (commentaries); Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* discusses selected rules at length.[^vedic][^mahabhashya] A sūtra is an atomic statement; the layers of commentary are its links.

The great mathematical notebooks of the subcontinent come from Srinivasa Ramanujan (1887–1920). From about 1903 he recorded his results in notebooks, without proofs. He was too poor to buy enough paper, so he worked on a slate and copied only the final results.[^ramanujan-arxiv] The three notebooks hold about 3,000–4,000 theorems.[^ramanujan-berndt] Bruce Berndt has spent his career, since 1977, proving them; one count gives 3,254 entries.[^ramanujan-proving][^ramanujan-imsc] A notebook of statements, each one small, has kept mathematicians busy for a century.

### Europe: commonplaces and slips

Europe had its own traditions. Commonplace books collected passages under headings. In the 1540s the naturalist Conrad Gessner cut his excerpts into slips and held them in order with temporary glue until he could rearrange them alphabetically.[^gessner] John Locke kept commonplace books from his first year at Oxford in 1652 and published an indexing method for them in 1685 (in English, 1706).[^locke] Leibniz admitted that he forgot what he had done within a few months; note systems were the remedy for that loss.[^vine]

Carl Friedrich Gauss kept a mathematical diary from 1796 to 1814: 146 short, dated entries, most of them cryptic Latin statements of results.[^gauss-wiki] Entry 1 records the construction of the regular 17-gon by ruler and compass; entry 18 reads "ΕΥΡΗΚΑ! num = Δ + Δ + Δ", every number is a sum of three triangular numbers.[^gauss-wikiwand] Of the 146 entries, 102 date from before 1800.[^gauss-msu] The diary was rediscovered in 1897 and published by Felix Klein in 1903.[^gauss-wiki]

## 3. Why mathematics and physics need this most

### The atoms already exist

Most fields must decide what "one idea" means. Mathematics decided long ago: a definition, a lemma, a theorem, a proof, an example, a remark. One public Zettelkasten of mathematics and physics labels its slips exactly so ("Defn: Lie Group", "Thm. …", "Pf: …").[^pqnelson] A mathematician on the Zettelkasten forum reports that proofs and sub-proofs fit naturally, because a link can be both a relation and an implication, and the backlinks of a note show its assumptions.[^zk-dissertation]

### Results need addresses that do not move

The Stacks Project, an open reference for algebraic geometry, gives every definition, lemma and theorem a four-character tag. The tag does not change when the result moves within the text; a result found to be wrong is removed, but its tag stays with an explanation.[^stacks] The tags were Cathy O'Neil's idea, introduced because numbering changes over time.[^dejong] The Lean library mathlib now tags its formal results with the matching Stacks tags.[^mathlib] A Zettelkasten applies the same principle to one person's knowledge.

### The trap: copying the textbook

The commonest complaint from mathematicians who try the method is that their notes merely copy the textbook, and that mathematics resists being split into linkable pieces.[^lesswrong-math] Another writer found mathematical notes more granular and less composable than other notes, with fewer surprises, but found definition notes very useful for assembling proofs.[^obsidian-manuel] The answers from the traditions above are the same: write in your own words (Gu Yanwu), read with a focus (Dasan), make up your own problems and solve them (the advice given in the same discussion), and keep only what is yours.

### Memory is a skill, and retrieval trains it

Practice testing produces long-term retention far better than rereading; this "testing effect" is one of the most robust results in the science of learning.[^testing-pmc] Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 study made it widely known.[^roediger] Spacing practice over time improves long-term retention further, even when massed practice feels better in the short term.[^bjork] For a student who must carry definitions and theorems for years, a note system that can test its owner is not a luxury.

### Physics: know what you do not know

As a graduate student at Princeton, preparing for his oral qualifying examination, Richard Feynman opened a fresh notebook titled *Notebook of Things I Don't Know About*. He used it to take his knowledge apart and put it back together.[^feynman] An open question is a note too, and often the most useful one.

## 4. The modern revival: voices from practice

> **How this section was gathered.** The tools used to write this essay could not open Reddit pages or YouTube comment threads. Reddit voices appear here only where another site quotes them, and YouTube appears through video descriptions and the creators' own written pages. The comments under videos are not included. Every voice below links to a page that a reader can check.

### A method that travelled east

Sönke Ahrens's *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017) started the modern revival. The publisher's description now reports more than 100,000 copies sold and translations into seven languages (an earlier version of the description said 10,000 copies).[^ahrens-sales] Several of the translations went to Asia within a few years: Korean in May 2021 (*제텔카스텐*),[^ahrens-editions] simplified Chinese in 2021 (*卡片笔记写作法*),[^ahrens-cn] Japanese in October 2021 (*TAKE NOTES!*),[^ahrens-jp] traditional Chinese in April 2022 (*卡片盒筆記*) and Thai in April 2025.[^ahrens-editions] The method came back to the cultures that had kept slips, cards and brush notes for centuries, and readers there noticed this.

- **China.** A writer for the Chinese tech magazine *Shaoshupai* (少数派) described about two years of team practice with the method. They found three problems that the book does not solve: fleeting notes with highlights stay useful, so discarding them was a mistake; permanent notes are almost never finished, because research thinking changes slowly; and research projects, with their failed attempts and repeated experiments, make project notes too valuable to throw away. Their answer was to give more weight to project notes and index notes.[^sspai]
- **China, back to Japan.** A Douban reviewer argues that most Chinese readers know the Luhmann method only as *二手知识* (second-hand knowledge) from influencers and one popular book, and that few of them have produced major work with it. The reviewer turned instead to the Chinese translation of Umesao's *Chiteki seisan no gijutsu* (《智识的生产技术》). From it they took the rule *一卡一事* (one card, one matter), and the practice of laying cards out to find connections.[^douban]
- **Korea.** The Korean publisher reports an especially enthusiastic response among IT developers.[^watcha] A Korean newsletter writer notes that the method did not come from nowhere: Umberto Eco described cards in *How to Write a Thesis*, and the Korean philosopher Kang Yu-won recommends card notes as a way to study in his books and lectures.[^seoulalien]
- **Japan.** Japanese readers of the translation keep their reading notes in linked wikis such as Scrapbox. In his notes on the book, the writer Kurasawa Tadahiro (Rashita) compares leafing through a Zettelkasten with leafing through cards, Umesao's practice, and concludes that under all the outer layers the key is to write down your own thoughts and refer to them again and again.[^rashita-takenotes] He also connects Matuschak's "atomic notes" directly to Umesao's rule of one card, one item.[^rashita]
- **India.** On zettelkasten.de, Sascha Fast coached a student, Ashish, in using the method for Hindu philosophy, which he presents as a good test of the method for complex, seemingly endless subjects. The main difficulty was atomicity, because every idea can be taken apart further.[^hindu]

### Mathematicians and physicists online

- **The problem, named.** Sascha Fast, co-founder of zettelkasten.de, wrote in a Reddit post that *"Math and the other hard sciences are particularly resistant"*. Mathematicians and physicists, he said, cannot tell what belongs in the box, because their work produces exceptionally many scratch notes.[^thinking-reddit] His answer: scratch work done with pen and paper (he calls it *thinking notes*) is part of understanding, like drafts in drawing. When you understand, you keep the result and can throw away most of the scratch work.[^thinking-post]
- **A paper box of about 8,000 slips.** Alex Nelson (pqnelson), whose Zettelkasten is mostly mathematics and physics,[^pqnelson] keeps it fully on paper: an estimated 7,920 slips in 2022. Each slip carries a register (*defn*, *thm*, *pf*, *rmk*, *ex*) and a title that states the result, such as "Convergent sequences are bounded". He writes links in red ink, and he does not let himself look at the original source while he writes a permanent note. If he needs to look, the literature note was not good enough.[^pqnelson-workflow]
- **1,613 notes on mathematics.** A mathematics student, Aaron Manning, reports 284,491 words over 1,613 notes in about two and a half years. He keeps all notes in one folder and uses contents pages instead of folders, for example a note on algebraic number theory that is only a list of links. Linking, he says, is why older notes continue to receive attention.[^manning]
- **A graduate student on YouTube.** A mathematics graduate student published a video, *Thoughts on using the Zettelkasten method for mathematics note taking*, about their experience with the method.[^holmes-video]
- **Notes in LaTeX.** Alfred Holmes built TeXNotes, a Zettelkasten written in LaTeX, so that atomic notes can be pasted straight into the draft of a paper with their references intact.[^texnotes]
- **A physics wiki.** *The Quantum Well* is a public set of linked notes on mathematics and physics. Its author wants to click from a physics topic down to the abstract mathematics under it, to build *"that engine below the cockpit"*.[^quantumwell]
- **Students on the Obsidian forum (2024).** A mathematics student in Germany asked whether "one definition per note" is the right approach. One reply recommends one idea per note, made by breaking a large chapter note into smaller notes. An electrical engineering master's student handwrites in lectures, transcribes later with LaTeX Suite, and says that rewriting forces engagement with the material. A third reply doubts that typed mathematics notes add value at all.[^obsidian-uni]
- **On LessWrong.** One reader who delayed trying the method later wrote that it significantly increased their productivity, at least for idea and proof generation.[^lesswrong-boost] Another warns that most typed formats make mathematical symbols harder to use than paper, especially if you want to invent your own notation.[^lesswrong-symbols]
- **Earlier voices.** A mathematician with a doctorate in applied mathematics writes that their whole approach to problem solving in a Zettelkasten comes from mathematical problem solving.[^zk-math] Another forum member found mathematics worked well in a Zettelkasten; diagrams were the only difficulty, so they work on paper first and transfer later.[^zk-dissertation] A bioinformatics student found the method hard to apply to mathematics: hard to split, hard to link, and too close to copying the textbook.[^lesswrong-math] Andy Matuschak's widely read principles ask for atomic, concept-oriented, densely linked notes, and admit that notes that are too small fragment the network.[^matuschak-atomic][^matuschak-linked]

### The skeptics

- On Hacker News, one commenter notes that the method's creator was an academic in the humanities (Luhmann was in fact a sociologist), and argues that technical and quantitative subjects with a clear structure gain less from linking.[^hn]
- On the Zettelkasten forum, a member points out that mathematics and physics need practice that notes cannot replace: they would rather do more exercises than wrestle with ideas in a box.[^zk-exercises] Another, asked about physics exam preparation, suggests recording the conceptual methods for solving problems, not countless worked exercises.[^zk-physics]
- Some reviewers of Ahrens's book complain that it explains *why* at length and *how* only briefly.[^goodreads]

### What the voices agree on

1. **Scratch work and permanent notes are different things.** Mathematics happens on paper first. A permanent note keeps the result in your own words, not every step that led to it.
2. **Mathematical writing gives the note types.** Definition, theorem, proof, remark and example are the units, again and again.
3. **Handwriting, then transcription, is a common workflow.** Students write by hand in lectures and type later, and the rewriting is where the learning happens.
4. **Contents pages work better than folders.** Several people independently replaced folders with notes that are lists of links.
5. **Notes do not replace exercises.** The method helps you keep and connect results and methods. It does not do the problems for you.

## 5. Why Musubi is built this way

| Musubi feature | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| One idea per note, with a title that states it | Umesao's one card, one item; Pāṇini's sūtras; Matuschak's atomic notes |
| Permanent timestamp IDs; renaming rewrites links | Luhmann's numbered cards; Stacks Project tags |
| `:::definition`, `:::theorem`, `:::proof` blocks and note types | The genres of mathematical writing; Nelson's registers (*defn*, *thm*, *pf*, *rmk*, *ex*) |
| Plain links, and typed links (`supports::`, `refutes::`, `depends-on::`) | Umesao and Kawakita: link, do not classify; the commentary and gloss traditions, which record *how* one text answers another |
| Mentions under each note, with unlinked mentions | Backlinks show the assumptions of a result |
| Plain notes for results; your paper, tablet or Supernote for scratch work; ink images through Archive | Thinking notes stay out of the box (zettelkasten.de); handwrite first, transcribe later (students on the Obsidian forum) |
| Structure notes that are lists of links | Contents pages instead of folders (Manning); index notes (the *Shaoshupai* team) |
| Inbox and quick capture for fleeting notes | Ibn al-Jawzi's hunting of thoughts; Umesao's notebook of discoveries; Feynman's notebook of what he did not know |
| *Question* notes and `Q:`/`A:` cards with spaced repetition | The testing effect and the spacing effect |
| Maintain tab: orphans, unresolved links, stale notes | Dasan: keep the grain, remove the chaff; Gu Yanwu: revise, and delete what is not yours |
| Structure notes and the graph | Khipu pendant cords; Luhmann's entry points; Kawakita's diagrams |
| Shapes and colours by type; your own types | Gu Yanwu's categories; but types are markers, not folders |
| Plain Markdown files in a folder you own | Gauss's diary, found again more than eighty years after its last entry; Ramanujan's notebooks; Luhmann's archive. Durable media outlive the software that made them |
| LaTeX first, with LaTeX Suite shortcuts | Mathematics is written in it |

### What Musubi does not do

Musubi has no folders for notes, because a folder is a classification decided in advance. It has no AI that writes notes for you, because the value of a note is the thinking needed to write it. It sends nothing over the network, because a lifetime of notes belongs to the person who wrote them.

## 6. How to start

1. **Capture without judging.** Use the inbox (Alt C). A fleeting note can be one line.
2. **Process daily.** Turn each fleeting note into a permanent note in your own words, or delete it.
3. **Write one result per note.** A definition, a theorem, an example, an argument, a question.
4. **Link before you leave.** Every new note links to at least one older note, and says why.
5. **Test yourself.** Ask the note a question (`Q:`/`A:`), and review what is due.

The knot is the unit. The ties are the knowledge.
